This is the shared review reference for the bubble ship: **measured Blender alternatives
for geometry**, followed by the **concept sheet for style**. Both layouts use the actual
bridge geometry, unchanged in scale, inside a sphere of radius 100 m. Mark prefers **seven major tiers**, with buildings, forests and other architecture within
them. The twenty-deck option is retained for comparison; the detailed radii and clearance
remain proposals.

## What the comparison establishes

The bridge floor is at **Z +82 m**, its main circular deck is **44 m across**, and the
actual needle reaches **Z +98 m**. The bubble is **200 m across**, centred at the ship
origin. Blender +Z is forward/up; −Z is aft/down. There is no occupied level above the
bridge. The three thin sphere circles are measurement guides, not a solid hull.

**A** follows the existing 25 m tier cadence: bridge, then +57, +32, +7, −18, −43 and
−68 m. The last two tiers extend the old four-tier blockout. **B** explores twenty decks,
8.5 m apart, ending at −79.5 m. Revision 2 replaces the old 55% radius rule with a **95 m radius inner spherical envelope**.
This gives every lower floor at least **5 m clearance to the 100 m bubble**, rather than a
gap that grows toward the equator. Deck widths are derived from the sphere, not the illustration.
The 1 m slab thickness and small edge lip are included in the fit:
`radius = sqrt(95² − max(Z², (Z−1)²)) − 0.08` metres.

The actual bridge is a deliberate exception in this study: its 22 m radius is much smaller
than the bubble cross-section at +82 m. It remains the existing decision platform above the
wider tiers; an expanded upper terrace would require a separate geometry choice.

Seven layers are major inhabited tiers, not seven single-storey floors. The 25 m cadence
leaves about 24 m between lower floor slabs before architecture and bracing. Buildings can
contain smaller storeys, and forests can occupy tall open volumes. The actual bridge ramp
and underside locally reduce the space beneath the bridge.

The lower floors are opaque slabs 1 m thick, with a tapered central spire beneath the
actual bridge. This is a **dimension and occlusion blockout**, not a structurally engineered
ship. Rooms, roofs, gardens, lifts, bracing and railings on lower levels are absent; they
will add occlusion. The 5 m clearance is for floor geometry only: roofs and tree canopies
need their own sphere checks at their actual heights. For a point at height `h`, its horizontal
radius must stay below `sqrt(100² − h²)`; using `sqrt(95² − h²)` also preserves the proposed
5 m buffer. Upper-tier buildings and trees need greater setbacks because the sphere narrows
overhead. The imported bridge rails and fronds are present. Invisible game
collision meshes are excluded. Bridge fronds have their existing attachments; speculative
lower-level growth has not been invented to fill the foreground.

## What you can see looking downward

From the interior eye, 4.11 m inside the edge, the centre ray at 30°, 45° and 60° down
hits the bridge floor in both layouts. A camera cannot see through that floor.
With the revised wider floors, at the rim eye, 0.7 m inside the edge, the 30°, 45° and
60° centre rays reach the next deck in A. In B the next deck is closer and is reached even
at 15° down. A's 15° centre ray still clears the modeled ship. Use the angle selector
above to compare the full images: a centre-ray result describes only the middle pixel,
not everything in the wide field of view.

These are ordinary geometric visibility studies. They do not simulate relativistic
aberration or boundary glow. They establish which physical structures are in a camera's
view before those optical effects are applied.

## How this relates to the game panorama

The current game composite uses two different projections: the panorama/sky camera looks
inward and about 53.6° upward, while the orthographic play layer looks downward at 14°.
Those layers are not a single physically coherent camera for arbitrary tilting and panning.
The measured views here instead render **all ship geometry through one perspective camera**.
They are a reference for choosing future camera behaviour, not a claim that the current
composite already produces these sightlines.

Play, foreground and panorama describe rendering distance and composition, not physical
ship storeys. Nearby rails and fronds can form foreground parallax, but every visible
member needs a real root and a position in the shared model. The central spire is one
continuous structure; it must not be duplicated in a panorama plate.
