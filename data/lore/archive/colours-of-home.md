Earth's pictures reach the ship as numbers riding a beam of light. Speed
changes the beam, not the numbers.

Flying away from home at 0.99c, the beam arrives stretched: each hour aboard
brings only 0.070888 of an hour of Earth's history, and the light that
carries it has been pulled far into the infrared, longer waves than any eye
can see. Turn for home at the same speed and everything is squeezed: Earth's
days pour in 14.107 times faster than you live them, on light pushed beyond
the violet.

Yet a sunset sent from home is still orange when it is decoded aboard, and a
blue sky is still blue. The colours were written into the message as numbers,
and numbers do not redden. What the motion changes is when the pictures come,
and the colour of the beam that carries them.

Only light you look at directly takes on the shift: the stars ahead turn
blue, the stars behind turn red. The ship's receivers follow the beam
wherever it lands, so home arrives slow or fast, but always in its own
colours.
