There is a light ahead of us that no star makes.

The whole universe is filled with the afterglow of its early heat: the cosmic
microwave background. At rest it is cold, barely above absolute zero, and
invisible. It comes from every direction equally.

Running into it changes that. Ahead, its temperature is raised by the same
Doppler factor that blues the stars. At 0.99c the forward background is
38.44 K, still far too cold to see. Above a Lorentz factor of 146.4 it passes
798 K and glows a dull red to dark-adapted eyes. From a Lorentz factor of 275.2
it is 1,500 K and plainly orange.

At 0.999999c, a Lorentz factor of 707.107, the sky's oldest light stands at
3,853.7 K, the colour of a candle flame or a cool star. It is not a sharp
disc. It is a glow centred exactly on the bow, and its temperature halves
0.0810° from the pole. Its warm heart is about as wide as a grain of rice held
at arm's length.

You are looking at light released before the first stars formed, made visible
by nothing more than how fast you are going to meet it.
