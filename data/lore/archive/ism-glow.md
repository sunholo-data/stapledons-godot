Space between the stars is not empty, and it is not the same everywhere. The
Sun sits just inside the Local Interstellar Cloud, where there are
0.2474 cm⁻³ hydrogen atoms: one in every four cubic centimetres. Around the
cloud lies the hot gas of the Local Bubble, sixty times thinner, at
3.9 × 10⁻³ cm⁻³. At walking pace that is nothing. At our speeds it is the
voyage's real enemy, and the route decides how much of it we meet.

Seen from the ship, the gas becomes an oncoming beam. In gas as thin as one
atom in ten cubic centimetres, at 0.99c it pushes on the bow with the force of
light from 163 suns. At 0.999999c, it is 1.66 × 10⁶ suns. If the wall
absorbed that energy as heat and shone it inward, no one aboard would survive
a single crossing.

It does not. A particle that meets a barrier it cannot climb bounces off it
elastically, like light from a mirror. The wall turns the gas aside without
being heated. What remains is drag: in that same thin gas, 23.3 N on our
sphere at 0.99c. The drive must work against it. Over the trip to Alpha
Centauri that costs 1.37 × 10¹⁷ J, the energy locked in 1.52 kg of matter.
Inside a cloud it costs more; in the hot bubble, far less.

The bounced protons stream ahead of us in a thin, fierce plume. A tiny
fraction of the impacts are not perfectly elastic, and their energy becomes
light. That is the boundary glow: faint, brightest at the bow. It brightens
as we enter a cloud and dims in the hot bubble. Crew who keep the Watch read
speed and weather in it.
