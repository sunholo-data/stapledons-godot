The space around the Sun is patchy. We fly through clouds a few light years
across, warm gas at about seven thousand kelvin with 0.2474 cm⁻³ hydrogen,
and between them the hot gas of the Local Bubble, a million degrees and
nearly empty at 3.9 × 10⁻³ cm⁻³. The Sun is just inside one cloud, the Local
Interstellar Cloud. Alpha Centauri lies beyond another, the G cloud, and
its light carries that cloud's hydrogen lines to us. The road
to Aldebaran passes through the Hyades cloud. Astronomers mapped these clouds
from the absorption lines they print on the light of nearby stars.

The clouds carry dust. Most grains are smaller than the wavelength of light,
but the Ulysses spacecraft, crossing the Sun's poles for sixteen years, caught
grains far larger: up to 8.98 µm in radius, grains of solid rock. At our speeds every grain arrives with the energy of a bomb
fragment. At ninety-nine point nine per cent of light speed, in a warm cloud, the
wall meets 1,842 s⁻¹ grains of a micrometre or more.
The wall turns them aside like the gas, and the fraction that is not elastic
flashes on the forward dome. In the warm clouds the flashes glitter under the
glow; in the hot bubble they are fewer, and each one stands out.

Clouds also set a limit. Holding a cruise speed means pushing against the
drag everywhere along it. In the warm clouds our drive can hold any speed up
to a Lorentz factor of 2,117. Denser gas lowers that limit. A route
the drive cannot hold is refused, and the captain chooses:
slow down, or go around by way of another star.

(The cloud outlines on the chart are digitised from the published sky maps.
Their depths remain a game approximation; so is the glow of a grain's impact
on the wall.)
