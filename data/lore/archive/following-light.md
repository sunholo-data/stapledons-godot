To keep its voice on you, Earth must aim at a ship crossing its sky at nearly
the speed of light. It sounds impossible. Distance makes it gentle.

A ship crossing Earth's sky at 0.99c, forty light-years out, moves Earth's aim
by only 1.418° a year. The Moon moves farther than that across your old sky in
an evening.

Earth has to turn fastest when you fly away from it almost along its line of
sight, just to one side. Then its light chases you along your path, and the aim
swings faster by your Lorentz factor. At 0.999999c, forty light-years out, that
is 2.773° a day. One light-year from home it is 110.9° a day. Even that is
slower than Earth's own turning, which every telescope on it already follows.

The hard part is not turning but knowing where to turn. The light Earth sees
from you left years ago, from a place you have long since passed. So Earth
never aims at what it sees. It aims where your committed course says you will
be when its light arrives, the way a hunter leads a bird. Earth's first laser
links to deep space already aimed ahead of their spacecraft [cite: NASA Deep
Space Optical Communications aboard Psyche, 2023]. This is why your plan must
reach home before Earth can follow you. Without it, Earth would be aiming at a
memory.
