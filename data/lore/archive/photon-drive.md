The wall lets out nothing but light. So the only way the ship can push on the
universe is to throw light behind it. Our drive is a photon drive.

Light carries momentum. To speed up, the ship emits a beam aft, and the recoil
carries it forward. Momentum is conserved exactly; nothing in the drive
cheats.

The cost is set by the ship's effective mass, the small tunable inertia the
bubble presents to the outside, and by a quantity physicists call rapidity.
Rapidity is the honest measure of speed near light: it keeps growing while
speed itself crawls toward the limit. At 0.99c the rapidity is 2.6467. At
0.999999c it is 7.2543.

For every kilogram of effective mass, one boost to 0.99c radiates
2.379 × 10¹⁷ J. One boost to 0.999999c radiates 6.520 × 10¹⁷ J, only 2.741
times as much, though the clocks aboard slow far more. Braking costs the same
again.

That is the strange economy of the voyage. The last sliver of the speed of
light, which buys the most time, costs surprisingly little energy. The real
price of speed is paid elsewhere: in the gas between the stars, and in the
years at home.
